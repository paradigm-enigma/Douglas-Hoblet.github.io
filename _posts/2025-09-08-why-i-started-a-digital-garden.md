Three weeks into sophomore fall, I realized my note-taking system was broken.

I had one Notion page per class, a Google Doc for UROP, a mess of TeX files
in `~/mit/psets/`, and a frankly embarrassing number of half-written ideas
in the Apple Notes app on my phone. Individually each was fine. Collectively,
every time I wanted to connect something from 6.006 to something I'd read about
for 6.1010, I had to remember where I put it — and I usually didn't.

## What a digital garden actually is

The term comes from [Maggie Appleton](https://maggieappleton.com/garden-history)
and a handful of other web gardeners. The idea: notes aren't finished essays.
They're plants. Some are seedlings you jotted down yesterday. Some are evergreens
you've been tending for years. You publish them anyway, because writing in public
forces you to get the thought at least half-right.

> A garden's topology is more intricate than stream. It's not a queue,
> it's a web.

## My rules

Three, so I can actually keep them:

1. **Every note is linkable.** If I'm thinking about LP duality and it reminds me
   of something from 18.06, I want a URL I can drop into a pset writeup.
2. **Half-baked is fine.** I mark notes `seedling`, `budding`, or `evergreen`.
   Seedlings can be three sentences and a question mark.
3. **The garden is the index.** No Notion, no Docs. If it's a durable idea,
   it goes here.

## Why publish it?

Three reasons that don't fit on a T-shirt:

- **Writing forces clarity.** I can't fake my way through explaining a
  matrix decomposition to the internet the way I can in a half-page pset scratch.
- **Future-me is an idiot.** Past-me can leave breadcrumbs by writing them down.
- **Sometimes a stranger emails you.** That's the rarest and best outcome.

If you're an MIT student thinking about doing the same — my template is on
[GitHub](https://github.com/{{ site.social.github }}) and takes about an
hour to fork and deploy.
